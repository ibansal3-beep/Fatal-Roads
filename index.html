BEFORE THE STORY / UNDERSTANDING THE DATA

What Does One Record Represent?

The original FARS data is a collection of individual fatal-crash records. Each item represents one crash involving at least one fatality, not one person or one trip.

From Crash Records to Visual Evidence

Items: Individual fatal crashes, identified by a crash case identifier.

Categorical attributes: State, weather, lighting, and rural/urban classification.

Temporal attributes: Month, weekday, and hour. Month and weekday are ordered cyclically; hour follows a repeating 24-hour cycle.

Quantitative attributes: Fatalities per crash and derived crash counts, fatality totals, and average fatalities per fatal crash.

Geographic attributes: Latitude and longitude for geocoded crashes, alongside state identifiers.

Transformations: Crash-level items are grouped into 51 state-level records, 12 monthly records, condition-category summaries, and 168 weekday-hour combinations (7 days × 24 hours). Each aggregate stores counts or other derived measures for its group.

Visual encodings: The map uses geographic position and color; the temporal charts use ordered positions and color intensity; the scatterplot uses quantitative positions and bubble properties. The Fatality Signature uses weekday rings, hourly angular positions, crash-count color, darkness bands, and severity markers.

Which Records Are Included?

The national dataset contains 36,297 fatal crashes and 39,254 fatalities. The national weekday-hour summary includes 36,044 crashes with usable time information.

The Fatality Signature uses a geocoded subset of 36,127 crashes; 35,894 have valid weekday and hour values for its radial cells.

Aggregated counts describe recorded events, not crash probability or individual driving risk. Traffic volume and distance traveled are not included as exposure denominators.

Where Do Fatal Crashes Occur?

Fatal crashes were recorded across the country, but their totals vary by state.

Finding 01 — Fatal Crashes Are Geographically Concentrated

Texas recorded 3,774 fatal crashes, followed by California (3,583) and Florida (2,931). Together, these three states accounted for approximately 28.4% of the 36,297 fatal crashes recorded nationwide in 2024.

These same three states also recorded the highest fatality totals. Select either map metric to investigate how the geographic distribution compares.

These are absolute counts, not rates adjusted for population, traffic volume, or vehicle miles traveled. Higher totals do not necessarily indicate greater individual driving risk.

Finding 02 — Fatal Crashes Concentrate in Particular Periods

October recorded the highest monthly total (3,369 fatal crashes), followed by August (3,342) and September (3,277). February had the lowest total (2,498). Monthly totals describe the number of crashes, not the crash rate per day.

At the weekday-hour level, Saturday from 8:00–8:59 PM recorded 420 fatal crashes, followed by Saturday from 11:00–11:59 PM with 414. Four of the five highest-count weekday-hour combinations occurred on Saturday evenings.

The monthly chart covers 36,297 fatal crashes. The weekday-hour summary covers 36,044 crashes with usable weekday and hour information; 253 records are excluded from that summary. These datasets have different denominators.

These counts do not measure driving risk. Differences in traffic exposure, trip frequency, and the number of days in each month are not accounted for.

What Conditions Are Recorded?

Finding 03 — Fatal Crashes Occur Under Many Conditions

Clear weather was recorded in 27,161 fatal crashes (74.8% of the national total). Rain was recorded in 2,455 crashes (6.8%). The prevalence of clear weather among fatal crashes does not mean that clear weather is more dangerous.

Daylight accounted for 16,386 fatal crashes (45.1%). Categories explicitly classified as dark accounted for 18,066 crashes (49.8%). The remaining records include dawn, dusk, unknown, and other lighting.

Urban locations accounted for 21,527 crashes (59.3%), compared with 14,442 (39.8%) classified as rural. Other or unreported classifications account for the remaining records.

Each condition summary covers 36,297 fatal crashes. Percentages use that national total and include unknown or unreported categories in the denominator.

Recorded conditions describe circumstances, not causes. Without exposure data showing how much driving occurred under each condition, these counts cannot establish comparative driving risk.

CHAPTER 04 / STATE COMPARISONS

Do More Fatal Crashes Always Mean More Fatalities?

Crash totals and fatality totals describe related, but different, quantities.

One fatal crash may involve more than one death. Comparing both measurements can reveal differences in average fatalities per crash.

Each bubble represents a state. Compare fatal crashes, recorded fatalities, and average fatalities per crash.

Finding 04 — Crash Totals and Fatalities per Crash Tell Different Stories

Texas recorded the most fatal crashes (3,774) and fatalities (4,160), averaging 1.102 fatalities per fatal crash. California recorded 3,583 fatal crashes and 3,876 fatalities, averaging 1.082 fatalities per crash.

Nebraska recorded substantially fewer fatal crashes (223), but averaged 1.126 fatalities per fatal crash. Wisconsin recorded 529 fatal crashes and averaged 1.125 fatalities per crash. Both averages exceeded those of Texas and California.

Each bubble represents one of 51 jurisdictions (the 50 states and District of Columbia). Average fatalities per fatal crash equals recorded fatalities divided by recorded fatal crashes. The national dataset contains 36,297 fatal crashes and 39,254 fatalities.

These averages describe outcomes among fatal crashes, not the probability of experiencing a crash or dying while driving. Differences in crash counts and sample sizes also affect how precisely state averages can be compared.

THE TAKEAWAY

A National Total Hides Distinct Patterns

In 2024, the United States recorded 36,297 fatal crashes and 39,254 fatalities. Those totals are the starting point, not the complete story.

Geographically, Texas, California, and Florida together accounted for approximately 28.4% of recorded fatal crashes nationwide.

Temporally, four of the five highest-count weekday-hour combinations occurred on Saturday evenings. Recorded conditions also challenged simple assumptions: 74.8% of fatal crashes occurred in clear weather.

State comparisons revealed another distinction: Texas had the highest number of fatal crashes, but Nebraska and Wisconsin recorded higher average fatalities per fatal crash.

The Fatality Signature brings these ideas together by combining temporal concentration, lighting, and fatalities per crash into a single visual profile. Its purpose is to support interpretation, not to rank states or times by driving danger.

What Can We Conclude?

Fatal crashes are unevenly distributed across locations, times, and recorded conditions. However, these counts do not establish causation or individual driving risk. Comparisons of risk require additional exposure data, such as vehicle miles traveled or traffic volume.
